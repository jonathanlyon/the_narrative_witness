# Audience hypotheses

**Status:** Foundation hypothesis, not validated segmentation  
**Operational lens:** Aotearoa New Zealand and Australia; English-language product with culturally specific obligations  
**Research date:** 10 August 2026

## What we know, and what we do not

This document is a recruitment and learning instrument. It does not claim that “memoir writers” form one coherent market, or that age predicts motivation, ability, culture, or comfort with AI.

### Evidence

- There is established demand for guided life-story products that reduce initiation friction through weekly prompts, voice capture, family participation, and a printed outcome. Storyworth, Remento, Storii, and Meminto all sell versions of that journey. Their positioning is evidence of an active category, not evidence that their customers want a literary writing practice. [Storyworth](https://help.storyworth.com/en_US/getting-started/what-is-a-storyworth-memoir-subscription), [Remento](https://help.remento.co/en/articles/8365873-what-is-remento-and-how-does-it-work), [Storii](https://www.storii.com/faqs), [Meminto](https://meminto.com/)
- Local writing infrastructure exists across courses, workshops, mentoring, and writing groups. NZSA describes memoir teaching around point of view, time, structure, ethics, permissions, and publication; Faber Writing Academy offers an eight-week online memoir course covering memory, scene, voice, structure, and the complexities of writing a life. These are signals that writers need both craft and human context, not just capture. [NZSA Writer Toolkit](https://authors.org.nz/writer-toolkit/), [Faber Writing Academy](https://faberwritingacademy.com.au/course/writing-memoir-online-2026/)
- Aotearoa is ageing: 1,125,003 people, or 22.5% of the 2023 census usually resident population, were 60 or older. That makes later-life writers relevant, but it does not justify designing the product as an “elderly reminiscence” service. [Stats NZ](https://www.stats.govt.nz/information-releases/2023-census-population-dwelling-and-housing-highlights/)
- Digital access and confidence cannot be assumed. Stats NZ has documented lower household internet and mobile access among disabled people and declining access at older ages in its 2018 data. This evidence is dated and household-level, so it should shape accessibility testing rather than be used to stereotype individuals. [Stats NZ disability report](https://www.stats.govt.nz/assets/Uploads/Reports/Measuring-inequality-for-disabled-New-Zealanders-2018/Download-data/Measuring-inequality-for-disabled-New-Zealanders-2018-Stats-NZ.pdf)
- Memoir data may concern whānau, iwi, communities, and future generations as well as the writer. Te Mana Raraunga’s principles foreground authority, whakapapa/provenance, collective obligations, consent, respect, and guardianship. These principles require product and research choices beyond an individual privacy checkbox. [Te Mana Raraunga principles](https://www.temanararaunga.maori.nz/principles-of-maori-data-sovereignty), [principles brief](https://www.temanararaunga.maori.nz/s/TMR-Maori-Data-Sovereignty-Principles-Oct-2018.pdf)
- New Zealand’s Privacy Commissioner warns that generative AI can produce confident errors and that people retain rights to access and correct personal information. Australia’s OAIC advises against putting sensitive personal information into publicly available generative-AI tools. A memoir cohort therefore needs explicit AI disclosure, correction, and safe handling. [NZ Privacy Commissioner](https://www.privacy.org.nz/resources-and-learning/a-z-topics/ai/generative-artificial-intelligence/), [OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products/_nocache)

### Unknowns to validate

- Which threshold events create sufficient urgency to sustain a weekly writing practice?
- Do writers primarily seek a manuscript, a private act of meaning-making, a family object, or some combination?
- Which writers welcome AI interpretation, which accept only clerical help, and which require an AI-free path?
- Does the “Witness” relationship create more value than a course, editor, writing group, or general chatbot?
- How much provenance detail is reassuring before it becomes cognitively heavy?
- What do Māori writers and other culturally situated writers require for authority, collective consent, data location, and use of whakapapa or mātauranga? This cannot be inferred by a non-Māori product team.
- What level and structure of founder facilitation are safe and economically sustainable?

## Audience clusters

Clusters describe circumstances, not fixed identities. One person may move between several during a project.

| Cluster | Situation and threshold | Core job to be done | Likely barriers | What must be tested |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Not-yet writer** | Has stories, notes, or a recurring preoccupation but does not yet call themselves a writer | “Help me begin without making me perform expertise or commit to a whole book.” | Blank-page fear, shame about skill, unclear scope, AI suspicion | Whether gathering fragments and receiving a provisional reading creates momentum without taking authorship away |
| **Threshold writer** | A death, diagnosis, separation, migration, retirement, discovery, anniversary, or public event makes writing feel newly necessary | “Help me turn urgency and scattered material into a practice I can sustain.” | Emotional intensity, time pressure, privacy, uncertainty over what belongs to whom | Whether pacing, boundaries, and continuity matter more than feature breadth |
| **Archive bearer** | Holds letters, photographs, recordings, journals, research, or inherited papers | “Help me understand what this material is asking of me while preserving its source and context.” | Volume, formats, rights, provenance, family disagreement, fear of damage or loss | Whether source-linked sensing is more valuable than simple transcription or tagging |
| **Legacy storyteller** | Wants family or future generations to receive stories in an enduring form | “Help me make a meaningful record that still sounds and feels like me.” | Technology confidence, stamina, accessibility, pressure from gift purchaser, print logistics | Whether literary agency is desired, or whether a prompt-to-book service better serves this job |
| **Hybrid memoirist** | Has drafts across essays, journals, research, audio, newsletters, or an abandoned manuscript | “Help me see the work as a whole and discover its next form without flattening it.” | Tool fragmentation, version drift, over-abundance, frustration with generic advice | Whether cross-Idea linking, versioning, and source-grounded synthesis materially improve progress |
| **Identity or testimony writer** | Is writing across adoption, disability, migration, class, gender, violence, institutional harm, whakapapa, or contested history | “Help me make meaning without pathologising, extracting, or overruling my account.” | Safety, exposure, legal/relational risk, cultural authority, AI bias, therapeutic misframing | Whether the Witness can hold ambiguity and writer authority under emotionally and politically complex conditions |
| **Witness-seeking writer** | Can write alone but lacks a sustained, attentive reader who remembers the project | “Help me stay in relationship with the work and hear what may be gathering within it.” | Isolation, inconsistent feedback, premature critique, costly editorial help | Whether a continuous Witness relationship produces stronger persistence and decisions than episodic feedback |

## Working pen portraits

These portraits are deliberately composite and fictional. They are prompts for recruitment, not personas to optimise around before evidence exists.

### Mara — the archive bearer

Mara, 54, works four days a week in Wellington. After clearing her mother’s house, she has boxes of photographs, letters, and cassette recordings alongside her own notes about adoption and family silence. She is competent with Google Drive but does not trust a chatbot with the archive. She can work for two focused hours on Sunday and in small bursts during the week.

- **Job:** build a traceable body of material, notice connections, and decide what story is hers to tell.
- **Success:** she can see where every interpretation came from and can set aside material that is not hers to use.
- **Failure:** the system confidently resolves contradiction, turns family pain into uplift, or loses the context of a source.

### Ben — the threshold writer

Ben, 39, lives in Melbourne and has written fragments since a serious diagnosis. He reads memoir and wants literary quality, but the idea of “writing his life” feels grandiose. He uses voice notes on walks, writes in a phone app, and has tried a general AI chatbot. Its polished prose felt falsely fluent.

- **Job:** convert urgency into a manageable writing practice without outsourcing the sentences.
- **Success:** a weekly rhythm and a small number of alive, writer-chosen directions.
- **Failure:** encouragement becomes pressure, the AI writes in his voice, or the product implies therapy.

### Aroha — the culturally accountable storyteller

Aroha, 63, is gathering stories that cross personal memory, whakapapa, and whānau history. Some material can be shared with descendants; some is restricted; some requires discussion before any digital use. She is comfortable online but wants to know where material goes, who can see it, and whether an AI provider retains it.

- **Job:** write with individual agency while maintaining obligations to people, whakapapa, and tikanga.
- **Success:** granular control, provenance, meaningful consent, and a product team willing to be governed rather than merely “consult.”
- **Failure:** individual consent is treated as sufficient authority or cultural material becomes model input by default.

### June — the legacy storyteller

June, 72, in regional New Zealand, wants grandchildren to know about a life they never saw. Her daughter has offered a prompt-to-book gift. June likes talking but also wants to choose words carefully; arthritis makes long typing difficult. She uses email and video calls but dislikes new accounts.

- **Job:** preserve stories accessibly and in her own terms, with family connection and an eventual object.
- **Success:** voice capture, readable editing, no hidden automation, and human help when stuck.
- **Failure:** a family purchaser controls the project, accessibility is added late, or “no writing required” erases her wish to craft.

### Theo — the hybrid memoirist

Theo, 46, in Sydney, has 40,000 words, a newsletter archive, interviews, and many versions of an outline. He has attended a course and does not need introductory prompts. His problem is abundance: he cannot see what the book is becoming.

- **Job:** make structural and thematic decisions while preserving complexity and source history.
- **Success:** sensing reveals supported patterns and productive questions, then gets out of the way of drafting.
- **Failure:** a rigid memoir template or generic “show, don’t tell” feedback.

## The first cohort: twelve writers as a learning system

The cohort should deliberately mix motivations, material states, technology confidence, and AI stance. It should not attempt demographic representativeness with twelve people. The aim is to expose consequential differences early.

### Recruitment frame

- 2 not-yet or early threshold writers.
- 2 archive bearers, including at least one with significant non-text material.
- 2 legacy-oriented storytellers, including one participant who benefits from accessibility adaptation.
- 2 hybrid memoirists with substantial existing material.
- 2 identity/testimony writers whose work tests ambiguity, privacy, and authorial authority.
- 2 flexible places used to correct gaps after screening.

Recruit across Aotearoa and Australia, aiming for variation in age, rural/urban setting, ethnicity, income, disability, writing experience, and AI stance. Do not recruit Māori participation as a diversity credential. If the cohort includes Māori writers or Māori data, agree governance, storage, access, use, and review with appropriately authorised Māori partners before intake.

### Minimum safety and participation conditions

- Clear statement that the programme is literary support, not therapy, crisis care, legal advice, or a promise of publication.
- Writer controls what enters the system, what AI can process, what is shared, and what is deleted.
- An AI-free or AI-minimal route through Gather and Create is available.
- No participant must read work aloud, disclose trauma, or give peer feedback to remain in the cohort.
- Human contact and an escalation path exist for product harm, privacy concerns, or distress; the founder is not positioned as a clinician.
- Participation is not conditional on granting marketing, model-training, or quotation rights.
- Access needs and technology support are gathered privately before the cohort begins.

## Jobs, desired progress, and product response

| Moment | Functional progress | Emotional/social progress | Appropriate product response | Inappropriate shortcut |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Before beginning | Gather without committing to a book | Feel legitimate and unhurried | Small capture paths, private orientation, reversible choices | “Write your memoir in a weekend” promise |
| Material accumulates | Find and relate fragments | Replace overwhelm with curiosity | Ideas, references, provenance, writer-invoked Sense | Automatic master outline presented as truth |
| Meaning emerges | Decide what may belong together | Feel accompanied without being interpreted over | Provisional observations, source links, questions, corrections | Psychological diagnosis or certainty about motive |
| Drafting begins | Move selected context into sustained prose | Protect confidence and voice | Stable Writing Pad, revisions, chosen prompts, quiet Witness | One-click prose generation in the writer’s voice |
| Work meets readers | Choose audience and boundaries | Retain agency under exposure | Consent-aware sharing and optional facilitation | Default social feed or compulsory critique |
| Work approaches form | Prepare an edition or next step | See the work as real without forcing publication | Export, editorial handoff, later Press pathways | Automated publication before authorial readiness |

## End-to-end audience journeys to test

### Journey A: from scattered fragments to an Idea

1. Writer enters with three pieces of material and no declared thesis.
2. They create or name an Idea, or allow the name to emerge later.
3. They link rather than duplicate material that belongs in more than one inquiry.
4. They invoke Sense on an explicit source set.
5. They inspect the reading, open “This came from,” correct it, and preserve one Story Seed.
6. They return a week later and can immediately understand the state of the work.

**Acceptance signal:** the writer reports increased orientation without reporting that the system decided the story for them.

### Journey B: from voice capture to crafted prose

1. Writer records a short memory because typing is difficult or speech is the natural entry point.
2. Original audio and verbatim transcript remain available.
3. The writer corrects transcription before interpretation.
4. Sense proposes questions and connections, not a polished substitute memoir.
5. The writer carries selected context into the Writing Pad and authors the passage.

**Acceptance signal:** ease of capture rises while perceived ownership remains high.

### Journey C: from sensitive archive to bounded inquiry

1. Writer marks source ownership, people named, sharing restrictions, and uncertainty.
2. The product prevents accidental cohort or AI exposure outside those settings.
3. The writer links only selected excerpts into an Idea.
4. Sense distinguishes source fact, writer assertion, and model inference.
5. Writer can remove a derived item without destroying the original archive.

**Acceptance signal:** the writer can explain who has authority over each layer and trusts the correction/deletion path.

## Interview and screening questions

Ask for examples rather than opinions about hypothetical features.

### Current behaviour

1. Tell me about the last time you felt you should write something from your life. What happened next?
2. Show me where relevant material lives now. What do you avoid opening, and why?
3. When did you last sustain a personal writing practice? What helped it continue or caused it to stop?
4. Who, if anyone, reads the work now? What kind of response is useful or harmful?
5. Have you paid for a course, editor, app, book service, transcription, or AI tool? What were you buying progress toward?

### Authority, privacy, and culture

6. Which parts of the material are yours to use, shared, uncertain, restricted, or not yours?
7. Who could be affected by the work even if it remains private?
8. What would you need to know before allowing an AI system to process it?
9. What should a system never infer, rewrite, share, or retain?
10. Are there cultural, whānau, community, legal, or safety protocols the programme must follow?

### Value and trade-offs

11. Which is more difficult now: beginning, organising, finding meaning, drafting, revising, sharing, or finishing?
12. If a thoughtful reader remembered everything you had supplied, what would you want them to do—and not do?
13. Would source links under an interpretation feel reassuring, distracting, or both? Show us on a prototype.
14. What would make a twelve-week programme worth the time? What would make you leave?
15. If the product disappeared tomorrow, what would you need to take with you?

## Falsifiable hypotheses and decision thresholds

| Hypothesis | Test | Evidence that supports it | Evidence that weakens it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Writers value being witnessed more than having prose generated | Compare a question-and-reflection flow with a polished-output flow using the same material | Most choose reflection, report higher ownership, and return to draft | Most choose generated prose and see reflection as friction |
| Traceability creates trust and better decisions | Usability test every sensing claim with visible source links | Writers check sources, correct claims, and report increased trust | Links are rarely used, poorly understood, or create false confidence |
| A named Idea is enough to begin sensing | Test sparse Ideas with 3–8 fragments and no formal thesis | Writers find the output relevant and non-prescriptive | Outputs are vague or writers need an explicit intent first |
| Mixed-format gathering unlocks writers blocked by text-first tools | Offer text, link, image/document, and voice entry | Participants adopt different modes and later write from them | Almost all use text and other modes add support burden without value |
| A mixed cohort strengthens learning without reducing safety | Track participation, belonging, and relevance by cluster | Cross-cluster differences create useful insight and no group is consistently marginalised | Needs conflict, participants self-censor, or facilitation becomes unsafe |
| Writers will pay for continuity and discernment rather than a printed gift | Conduct price/value interviews after lived use, not before | Participants name ongoing Witness/Sense value independent of print | Value is understood only when attached to a finished book |

## Research decisions for the foundation phase

### Borrow

- Low-friction prompts and voice entry from legacy-story services.
- The craft depth, ethical questions, and human holding found in memoir courses.
- Source grounding and visible citations as demonstrated by NotebookLM, while testing a writing-specific interaction rather than copying its research-notebook model. [NotebookLM help](https://support.google.com/notebooklm/answer/17003757)

### Avoid

- Treating older people, adoptees, Māori writers, disabled writers, or trauma writers as a single-product niche.
- Assuming family purchasers and writers have aligned interests.
- Equating capture volume, AI output, or a printed book with meaningful authorial progress.
- Using private memoir material as model-training, evaluation, or marketing data without separate informed consent.

### Test first

- The Witness tone and degree of intimacy.
- Whether Story Seeds feel distinct from a Sensing Document.
- How much source detail belongs in the primary interface.
- The workable weekly cadence, cohort duration, and human facilitation ratio.
- Which accessibility accommodations must be defaults rather than individual requests.

## Founder decisions required after discovery

1. Which primary progress promise can be made honestly after the cohort?
2. Who is the first paying customer: the writer, a family member, an organisation, or a facilitated programme purchaser?
3. What Māori governance relationship is required before accepting Māori data at cohort or commercial scale?
4. What level of emotional-risk screening and facilitator training is proportionate to a literary programme?
5. Which cohort needs are product requirements, which are facilitation requirements, and which should be referred elsewhere?
